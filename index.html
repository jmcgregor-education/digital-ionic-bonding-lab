Mini-lesson A: Binary ionic compounds

Cation first, anion second, and the charges must cancel to zero. Take Mg2+ and Cl−. One Mg gives +2, so you need two Cl−: MgCl2. For Al3+ and O2−, the common multiple is 6: two Al and three O make Al2O3. Always use the lowest ratio, so Ca2+ and O2− is CaO.

To name it, say the metal, then the nonmetal with the ending -ide: magnesium chloride. Ionic names never use di- or tri-.

Mini-lesson B: Polyatomic ions

A polyatomic ion is one unit with one charge. Do not split it up. Ca2+ and NO3− need two nitrates, so the formula is Ca(NO3)2. The parentheses show that the 2 applies to the whole ion. Use parentheses only when you need more than one: NaOH has none, Ca(OH)2 does.

The name keeps the polyatomic ion’s own name: calcium nitrate.

Mini-lesson C: Metals with more than one charge

Most transition metals, plus tin and lead, can have more than one charge. Their names need a Roman numeral that shows the metal’s charge. Groups 1 and 2, aluminum, zinc, and silver never get one.

Find the charge by balancing. In FeCl3, three Cl− make 3−, so the one Fe is 3+: iron(III) chloride. In Fe2O3, three O2− make 6−, shared by two Fe, so each is 3+: iron(III) oxide. The Roman numeral is the charge, not the number of atoms.
